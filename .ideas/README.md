# Anno 117: Pannonia
## Orchards, Riverlands and the Danube Frontier

*A regional expansion concept*

A fishing boat slips between reed-covered islands. Beyond the riverbank, a wooden road leads to a market where baskets of fruit stand beside woollen cloaks and freshly fired pottery. Further inland, horse breeders share the open countryside with shepherds, vineyards and orchards. Along the main road, carts carry grain towards a military granary. Across the water, a watchtower answers a distant signal.

This is Pannonia: a province where rural communities, growing Roman towns and the Danube frontier depend on one another.

The idea is to bring a new kind of regional economy to Anno 117. Pannonia would combine the wetland settlement and branching population structure associated with Albion with the urban institutions, engineering and trade of Latium. Its own identity would emerge through orchards, basket weaving, horse breeding, provincial craftsmanship and the organisation of a vast frontier supply network.

The player’s task would be to turn these different landscapes and communities into a prosperous province. A productive countryside would support ambitious towns; those towns would provide the infrastructure and commercial connections that allow the countryside to flourish. Eventually, the entire economy would contribute to the region’s defining undertaking: the **Ripa Pannonica**, a connected system of roads, forts, river harbours and supply stations along the Danube.

## A landscape that shapes the economy

The region would draw its setting primarily from the lands of present-day western Hungary: the Danube corridor, the countryside around Aquincum, the western trading routes through Savaria and Scarbantia, and the shores and hills around Lake Pelso, today’s Lake Balaton.

Water would give Pannonia its structure. River islands, sheltered backwaters, reed beds and floodplains would sit alongside dry farmland, wooded slopes and broad pastures. Settlements would grow where these landscapes meet.

Wetlands would offer fishing, eel catching and materials for basket weaving. Building there would require careful placement, wooden walkways and connections to firmer ground. The most convenient river landing might occupy the same stretch of shoreline that would otherwise support a productive fishing settlement.

Dry ground would carry a different set of opportunities. Grain fields would feed the population, sheep would supply wool, and horses would need generous pastures. Orchards and vineyards would make suitable hillsides valuable, while forests and clay deposits would support construction and manufacturing.

These different uses would create the region’s first meaningful decisions. A large meadow could support the horses needed by the army, the sheep needed by clothing workshops, or the livestock supplying urban food production. A fertile riverside plot might become a farm, a warehouse district or the approach to a major crossing.

The geography would encourage specialisation and trade between settlements. A wetland community could supply baskets and fish to an inland town. An orchard district could send fruit towards both local markets and urban preservation workshops. A riverside administrative centre could receive food, timber and horses from several smaller settlements.

Pannonia would become strongest when its different landscapes worked together.

## One population, two paths to prosperity

Everyone would begin as **Provinciales**, the ordinary provincial population: farmers, fishers, craftspeople, traders, settlers and military families establishing a livelihood.

From this shared starting point, two development paths would emerge:

```text
                         Provinciales
                         /           \
                     Vicani        Municipes
                       |               |
                   Principes       Decuriones
```

The **Vicani–Principes** path would develop through local communities, rural production, craftsmanship and regional leadership.

The **Municipes–Decuriones** path would develop through town life, civic institutions, administration and long-distance commerce.

These would represent different ways of organising prosperity within the same province. Both would take part in Roman provincial life, and both would contain people from different backgrounds. Their distinction would lie in their institutions, consumption and economic role.

The local path would favour productive estates, workshops and regional self-sufficiency. The municipal path would favour concentrated urban development, public services and the movement of goods through a larger imperial economy.

A successful province would benefit from maintaining both.

Population needs would preserve the flexibility of Anno’s category-based progression. Each tier would introduce new food, clothing, household and service options while retaining earlier needs. Players could build an effective combination of supplies suited to their territory, then broaden that provision for a richer and more prosperous population.

## Provinciales: establishing a home

The opening settlement would revolve around food, clothing and a small civic centre.

**Grain Porridge** would provide a dependable food source from dry farmland. **Freshwater Fish** would offer an alternative connected to the river landscape. Open riverbanks would support fishing huts, while marshy backwaters would favour eel-catching grounds. Both would contribute to the same basic freshwater-food need, allowing different starting locations to support different layouts.

A **Market** would bring the settlement together. Nearby, a small **Sacellum**, or roadside shrine, would provide its first religious service. Positioned beside a junction or on the edge of the village, it would establish the connection between everyday travel, community life and worship.

For clothing, residents would seek **Tunics** and **Woollen Cloaks**. Tunics would supply the basic clothing economy, while cloaks would introduce the importance of sheep and wool in a cooler, wetter frontier landscape.

The first hours would therefore establish two complementary parts of the settlement: a waterside economy of fishing and gathering, and a dry-land economy of grain, livestock and timber.

Even as the province developed into a network of wealthy towns, these communities would remain essential. Their food, clothing and labour would support everything built above them.

## Vicani: the prosperous local community

The first branch would lead to the **Vicani**, residents of growing local settlements whose wealth comes from the surrounding countryside and its workshops.

Their food needs would introduce two characteristic products: **Pannonian Grain Beer** and **Fruit Baskets**.

Beer would turn grain into a more valuable everyday drink. Fruit Baskets would establish a connection between two very different environments: orchards on suitable dry ground and basket-weaving workshops supplied by the wetlands.

The community would gain a **Vicus Hall**, a gathering place that supports local crafts and labour. A larger **Civitas Sanctuary** would give religious life a more prominent role, with a choice of patronage connected to activities such as woodland production, animal husbandry, fishing or craftsmanship.

Clothing would become more distinctive. **Braccae**, practical woollen trousers, would extend the textile economy. **Norico-Pannonian Fibulae**, decorative metal brooches used to fasten clothing, would bring bronze working into household prosperity.

Two household goods would complete the tier: **Pannonian Pottery** and **Woven Baskets**.

Pottery would turn local clay into cooking vessels and storage containers. Basket weaving would make reeds or willow a valuable industrial resource, supplying both homes and food production.

The Vicani would be the productive heart of the regional economy. They would offer strong local supply networks and relatively modest dependence on imported luxuries. Their settlements would fit naturally around farms, clay workings, woodland and waterways, creating prosperous communities beyond the main cities.

## Principes: wealth rooted in the province

The Vicani would develop into the **Principes**, the leading families and patrons of local communities.

Their prosperity would give the countryside a more sophisticated economy. Estates would support specialist workshops, better clothing, improved transport and prestigious public projects.

**Smoked Meats** would turn livestock into preserved food suitable for local consumption and long-distance supply. **Pelso Wine** would give vineyard districts around the lake a valuable regional product, allowing the player to develop a recognisable wine-growing landscape over the course of the game.

Public life would centre on a **Civitas Council House**. Here, regional leaders could support agriculture, craftsmanship, commerce or frontier provisioning through local policies.

A **Spring Bath and Healing Sanctuary** would connect health and worship to the landscape. Freshwater springs would become desirable locations for settlements whose prosperity expressed itself through a combination of local sacred places and Roman bathing culture.

Clothing would advance to **Fine Woollen Mantles** and **Amber Jewellery**. The mantles would refine the existing wool economy, while amber would introduce a deliberate trade dependency. Imported amber would arrive through western commercial connections and be worked locally into valuable ornaments.

Households would seek **Fine Pannonian Tableware** and **Riding Horses**. The former would support more specialised pottery workshops; the latter would give horse breeding an important civilian market alongside its transport and military roles.

The Principes would represent a wealthy provincial society built around land, craftsmanship, community institutions and regional trade.

## Municipes: the rise of the town

The second branch would lead from Provinciales to the **Municipes**, a population whose ambitions centre on urban life.

Their food economy would introduce **Bread** and **Garum**. Mills and bakeries would support a concentrated population, while fish-sauce production would connect freshwater resources with salt supplies and processing workshops.

Their public services would immediately change the character of the settlement.

**Public Baths** would require investment in water, fuel and urban space. A **Mansio**, or road station, would serve travellers and support the movement of carts and goods. Its location would matter: a busy approach road or a junction between a harbour, town and agricultural district would give it a practical role in the wider economy.

Residents would wear **Sandals** and **Fine Tunics**, supporting leatherworking, textiles and finishing workshops.

Their household needs would be **Amphorae** and **Terracotta Oil Lamps**. Amphora production would connect the town to the transport of wine, oil and other processed goods. Lamp workshops would create another use for clay, while a reliable olive-oil supply could offer an additional household benefit.

The municipal path would reward investment with a stronger tax base and more concentrated urban activity. In return, it would demand more services, processing and transport.

A growing town would need a countryside capable of feeding it and a trade network capable of supplying what the province could not readily produce.

## Decuriones: governing a wealthy province

The municipal path would culminate in the **Decuriones**, the civic elite whose wealth and influence are expressed through administration, commerce and public building.

Their food needs would combine **Imported Vintage Wine** with **Dried and Honeyed Fruits**.

Imported wine would give established trade routes a continuing purpose, even after Pannonia developed its own vineyards. Dried and honeyed fruit would provide a more refined use for local orchard production, linking rural prosperity directly to elite urban consumption.

A **Forum and Basilica** would become the centre of civic business, taxation and administration. A **Grammaticus School** would support education and the literate workforce needed by prosperous towns and provincial government.

Residents would seek **Togas** and **Silver Fibulae**. These would extend existing textile and metalworking industries into prestige production.

Their household needs would include **Writing Tablets** and **Fine Glassware**. Tablets would connect wood and wax production to the work of merchants, officials and military administrators. Fine glass would bring another luxury trade or specialist manufacturing opportunity into the province.

The Decuriones would provide the income, knowledge and administrative capacity needed for the largest projects. Their prosperity would rest on the reliability of the entire regional economy.

## Orchards and baskets: a small industry with wide consequences

Orchards would be one of Pannonia’s most recognisable features, both visually and economically.

A modular **Orchard** would allow different planted groves: apples and pears, plums and cherries, followed by more developed walnut and peach plantings. These would provide variety in the landscape while sharing a manageable orchard resource and common processing network.

The harvest would have several destinations.

Fresh fruit would be packed into woven baskets for the Vicani. Fruit and honey would supply the richer dishes consumed by the Decuriones. Drying workshops could prepare provisions for military supply, ships and distant markets.

This would make orchard production relevant across population tiers and economic activities.

The basket would be just as important as the fruit. It would serve as a household good in its own right and as an input for the Fruit Basket chain. Expanding food production would therefore increase demand on the same weaving workshops that supplied homes.

A new orchard district might appear to be a straightforward agricultural expansion. In practice, it could require additional wetland gathering, more basket weavers and improved transport between the river and the hills.

That is the kind of interdependence at the centre of the concept: a simple, understandable product whose production draws several parts of the region together.

## Horses, transport and the military economy

Horse breeding would give Pannonia another major economic system.

Pastures, breeding farms and training paddocks would produce horses for three connected roles: **riding**, **draught work** and **military service**.

Riding horses would satisfy the Principes and support the prestige of prosperous rural estates. Draught horses would improve agricultural and transport activity. Military horses would supply auxiliary cavalry through a more demanding equipment chain.

Equipping cavalry would require more than breeding animals. Leatherworkers and metalworkers would produce tack, while textile and weapons workshops would supply cloaks and arms. The military economy would therefore draw on several existing industries.

Land use would become a strategic consideration. Horse pastures would compete with sheep, cattle and other agricultural demands. Expanding cavalry capacity could mean opening a new farming district or importing food to free local land for breeding.

Bow-making would provide a further connection between woodland resources, animal products and military production.

Across the Danube, relations with the **Iazyges** would introduce a diplomatic route to allied mounted troops. In the concept, a trade or alliance agreement would unlock Iazygian horse archers, supplied through horses and bows as well as a continuing political relationship.

The opposite bank would become an active neighbour: a place for negotiation, commerce and military cooperation.

## Trade: what Pannonia gives, and what it needs

Pannonia’s place in the wider empire would come from its ability to supply food, horses, clothing, pottery and military provisions.

Its exports could include preserved meats, woollen garments, baskets, trained horses, regional wine and finished goods made from imported materials. Amber jewellery would be a particularly clear example: foreign raw material, local craftsmanship and a valuable finished product.

Imports would help define the province just as strongly.

Olive oil would support urban households. Salt would serve food processing. Amber would arrive through long-distance trade. Silver and other valuable materials would feed prestige workshops, while vintage wines and Mediterranean fruits would satisfy wealthy consumers.

The two population branches would take different positions within this network. Local communities would provide much of the food, labour and raw material. Municipal centres would concentrate processing, administration and access to distant markets.

For the player, trade planning would involve both regional and interregional routes. A river settlement might supply baskets to an orchard district, which then supplied fruit products to a city. That city might import oil and metal, distribute finished goods inland and send military supplies towards the frontier.

The result would be a province whose prosperity could be understood by following its roads and waterways.

## Faith and public life

Religion would develop alongside the economy, from small roadside shrines to major sacred districts.

The first Sacellum would belong to the everyday landscape of a young settlement. Later sanctuaries would allow communities to choose a patronage that suited their surroundings and priorities.

A woodland settlement might favour animal husbandry and forest production. A river community might invest in a sanctuary associated with water and fishing. A workshop town might support craftsmanship, while a community closely connected to the frontier could emphasise military service.

These choices would give settlements a character beyond the goods they produced.

Public institutions would reinforce the distinction between the population paths. Vicus halls and civitas councils would support local organisation and productive communities. Baths, road stations, schools and forums would serve the growing demands of urban life.

By the late game, the province would contain several kinds of prosperous settlement, each shaped by its landscape, population and civic priorities.

## Four Marvels, four expressions of prosperity

Each advanced population path would have two major Marvels.

For the **Principes**, the first would be the **Sacred Precinct of Gorsium**: a ceremonial centre of shrines, gathering spaces and festivals. Its role would be to bring communities together and reward investment in religious and civic life.

Their second would be the **Aquincum Spring and Bath District**. Developed around natural water sources, it would combine springworks, an aqueduct, baths and sacred spaces. Its construction would unite the landscape with engineering, making health and public wellbeing a visible achievement.

For the **Decuriones**, the **Governor’s Palace at Aquincum** would express the province’s administrative importance. Connected to the river and its transport network, it would serve as a prestigious centre of government, logistics and imperial relations.

The **Forum and Capitolium of Savaria** would express another kind of success: a wealthy civic centre sustained by trade, public institutions and urban development. Its monumental spaces would form the heart of a town connected to the western commercial routes.

Together, these projects would give the late game a varied architectural identity: sacred precincts, waterworks, government buildings and monumental civic spaces.

## Ripa Pannonica: building an entire frontier

Above the individual Marvels would stand the region’s largest undertaking: the **Ripa Pannonica**.

This would be a construction programme spread across the province. Instead of concentrating all late-game investment in one site, it would ask the player to connect a series of strategic locations into a functioning frontier.

The project would begin with a **limes road and milestones**, establishing the main supply corridor. **Watchtowers and signal stations** would extend its reach. **Auxiliary forts** would create military positions, followed by **river harbours and crossings** that connect land transport with the Danube.

**Granaries and supply stations** would turn the route into a dependable provisioning network. The later stages would develop **Brigetio’s legionary fortress** and **Aquincum’s command and logistics centre** as its principal anchors.

Both population paths would contribute.

The provincial communities would supply grain, meat, woollen cloaks, baskets, timber, horses and labour. The municipal centres would provide bricks, metalwork, amphorae, writing tablets, money, engineers and administrative capacity.

The project would give mature industries a continuing purpose. The basket workshops established during the growth of the Vicani would supply the frontier. Horse farms serving the Principes would help equip its troops. The schools and offices supporting the Decuriones would contribute the administrative resources needed to coordinate it.

Completion would bring practical rewards: faster transport, expanded storage and harbour capacity, improved frontier security, imperial purchasing contracts for military goods and new commercial agreements.

The achievement would be visible across the map. Roads would join previously separate settlements. Granaries would fill beside the river. Forts would receive regular shipments. Goods would move through a network the player had spent the entire game building.

## A province that grows with the player

The different locations would give this journey its landmarks.

**Aquincum** would grow around government, military logistics, springs and the river. **Brigetio** would anchor frontier manufacturing and military supply. **Savaria** would embody civic wealth and western commerce, while **Scarbantia** would serve as a gateway for amber and overland trade.

**Gorsium** would develop into a centre of community and sacred life. **Pelso** would offer a gentler landscape of fishing settlements, orchards, vineyards, pastures and increasingly prosperous estates.

The player would begin with a market, a few fishing boats and fields on dry ground. Over time, those modest beginnings would become a province of interdependent villages, towns, estates and military centres.

Pannonia’s appeal would lie in seeing how those parts fit together. Reeds become baskets; baskets bring fruit to market. Orchards support both rural households and urban luxury. Horses carry wealthy residents, move agricultural goods and equip cavalry. The town’s prosperity pays for roads that make distant farms more productive. The frontier provides a market for the industries that sustain it.

**Pannonia would be a region about building prosperity through connection: between water and land, countryside and town, local communities and imperial institutions.**

Its final achievement would be a living province along the Danube, with every orchard, workshop, harbour and road contributing to the whole.
